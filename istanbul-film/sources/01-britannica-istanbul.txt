Istanbul, largest city and principal seaport of Turkey. Historically known as Byzantium and then Constantinople, it was the capital of the Byzantine Empire and the Ottoman Empire. Istanbul straddles the Bosporus strait, one of two waterways that separates the European and Asian parts of Turkey.

The old city contains about 9 square miles (23 square km), but the present municipal boundaries stretch a great deal beyond the original peninsular city, situated on seven hills, requisite for Constantine's "New Rome." Six are crests of a long ridge above the Golden Horn; the other is a solitary eminence in the southwest corner. Around their slopes are ranged many of the mosques and other historic landmarks that were collectively designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1985.

By long tradition, the waters washing the peninsula are called "the three seas": they are the Golden Horn, the Bosporus, and the Sea of Marmara. The Golden Horn is a deep drowned valley about 4.5 miles (7 km) long. The Bosporus (Istanbul Bogazi) is the channel connecting the Black Sea (Karadeniz) to the Mediterranean (Akdeniz) by way of the Sea of Marmara (Marmara Denizi) and the straits of the Dardanelles.

Founding: The city was founded as Byzantium, probably around 657 BCE, by Greek colonists from Megara, led according to tradition by a man called Byzas. The colonists chose the site for its superb natural harbor (the Golden Horn) and strategic location commanding the strait between the Black Sea and the Sea of Marmara.

Roman Period: In 196 CE, the city was destroyed by the Roman emperor Septimius Severus as punishment for having supported a rival claimant to the throne. Severus quickly rebuilt it, however, adding the first city walls and constructing a new hippodrome. In 330 CE, the emperor Constantine the Great made it the new capital of the Roman Empire, calling it Nova Roma (New Rome), though it soon became known as Constantinople (City of Constantine). After the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476 CE, Constantinople continued as the capital of the Byzantine (Eastern Roman) Empire.

Byzantine Era: Under the emperor Justinian I (reigned 527-565), who rebuilt the great church of Hagia Sophia, Constantinople became one of the world's greatest cities. The Hagia Sophia was completed in 537 CE. Constantinople served as the preeminent trade hub between Europe and Asia, controlling the Bosphorus shipping lane that was the sole maritime route between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean.

Ottoman Conquest: The Byzantine Empire gradually declined, and on May 29, 1453, Constantinople fell to the Ottoman Turks under Sultan Mehmed II (called the Conqueror). Mehmed made the city the new capital of his empire. He converted the Hagia Sophia to a mosque and worked to repopulate and revitalize the city, which he called Istanbul. The Grand Bazaar (Kapali Carsi), begun under Mehmed II and expanded by Suleiman the Magnificent (reigned 1520-1566), became one of the world's greatest marketplaces, with thousands of shops.

Ottoman Trade: Under Suleiman the Magnificent, the city reached the height of its power and wealth. The architect Mimar Sinan designed dozens of magnificent buildings, including the Suleymaniye Mosque (completed 1557). Istanbul controlled the overland Silk Road terminus and maritime trade routes connecting the Black Sea grain trade with Mediterranean commerce. The city's strategic position astride the Bosphorus allowed the Ottoman Empire to collect tolls and regulate trade between Europe and Asia.

Modern Era: After World War I and the collapse of the Ottoman Empire, Mustafa Kemal Ataturk established the Republic of Turkey in 1923, and the capital was moved to Ankara. Istanbul remained Turkey's economic and cultural capital. In 1973, the Bosphorus Bridge (now the 15 Temmuz Sehitler Koprusu) was completed, linking Europe and Asia by road for the first time. The Marmaray rail tunnel beneath the Bosphorus opened in 2013, creating a continuous rail link between the European and Asian sides. Today Istanbul is Turkey's largest city with a population exceeding 15 million people and remains a global hub for trade, culture, and tourism. The Historic Areas of Istanbul were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1985.
