Historic Areas of Istanbul
UNESCO World Heritage List, Inscribed 1985

Outstanding Universal Value

Istanbul is the only city in the world situated on two continents — Europe and Asia. Its history covers over 2,500 years. The Historic Areas of Istanbul were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1985, meeting criteria (i), (ii), (iii), and (iv).

The site includes four areas: the Archaeological Park (including the Hippodrome of Constantine, the Hagia Sophia, the Topkapi Palace), the Süleymaniye quarter, the Zeyrek quarter, and the land walls of Constantinople.

Criterion (i): Istanbul contains outstanding examples of masterpieces of human creative genius. The Hagia Sophia (Ayasofya), completed in 537 CE under Emperor Justinian I, was the world's largest cathedral for nearly a thousand years. Its innovative pendentive dome was a milestone in the history of architecture. The Süleymaniye Mosque, designed by architect Mimar Sinan and completed in 1557, is considered his masterpiece and one of the finest expressions of Ottoman architecture.

Criterion (ii): Istanbul illustrates the decisive influence of Byzantine and Ottoman civilizations on the development of architecture, arts and human values. The Theodosian Land Walls, built between 412 and 422 CE under Emperor Theodosius II, defined the limits of Constantinople and helped protect it from invaders for over a thousand years.

Criterion (iii): Istanbul bears exceptional testimony to Byzantine and Ottoman civilizations. The Hippodrome was Constantinople's social centre for 1,000 years. The Grand Bazaar (Kapali Carsi), begun in 1461 under Mehmed II, is one of the largest and oldest covered markets in the world.

Criterion (iv): Istanbul is an outstanding example of an architectural ensemble that illustrates significant stages in human history — from the Greco-Roman city of Byzantium, through the Byzantine Christian Constantinople, to the Ottoman Islamic Istanbul.

Transport and Trade History:
The Bosphorus Strait, which runs through the heart of Istanbul, has been a critical trade and transport route since antiquity. The Golden Horn provided one of the finest natural harbors in the ancient world, enabling Constantinople to become the preeminent commercial city of its age. The city served as the western terminus of the Silk Road, where goods from China, India and the East were exchanged for European products. Control of the Bosphorus allowed successive rulers to levy tolls on all shipping between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean.

The Marmaray Project, completed in 2013, created a rail tunnel beneath the Bosphorus, linking the European and Asian rail networks of the city for the first time in history — a modern echo of Istanbul's timeless role as the bridge between continents.
